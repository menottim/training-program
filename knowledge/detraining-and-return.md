# Returning From a Training Layoff (Detraining and Retraining)

## Scope

Covers what happens to strength and muscle when resistance training stops, how much comes back and how fast, and how to set restart loads when returning to a lift after a gap. Calibrated for Menotti, a 40-year-old in-season recreational athlete who has now had three programming-relevant layoffs. Covers both travel gaps and injury layoffs. Does __not__ cover the calf rehab progression itself (see [calf-strain-rehab.md](calf-strain-rehab.md)), deload strategy in a non-fatigued lifter (see [in-season-volume.md](in-season-volume.md)), or pre-competition tapering.

## Athlete context

Three layoffs have driven programming decisions so far:

- __Weeks 3-4 (Mar 22 - Apr 4, 2026):__ travel, roughly a 20-day gap from barbell work, hotel bodyweight sessions only.
- __Week 5 (Apr 5-11, 2026):__ a re-baseline "push week" after that gap, which also introduced barbell back squat and barbell bench press.
- __Weeks 16-21 (Jun 21 - Jul 27, 2026):__ acute left medial gastrocnemius strain, then travel. The upper-body line was preserved and kept progressing throughout (bench went 155 to 165 during the layoff), while barbell lower (trap bar deadlift, back squat) went untouched from 6/18 to 7/27 - __39 days__.

Restart loads were set ad hoc on each occasion. This file exists so that they are not.

## Key Findings

| Claim | Evidence tier | Primary source |
|---|---|---|
| Training cessation reduces every component of muscular performance, with a dose-response on how long the break lasts: submaximal strength SMD -0.62, maximal force -0.46, maximal power -0.20. | Under about 4 weeks off ("short-term detraining"), training-induced gains in fibre cross-sectional area are reversed, but measured strength declines are limited. | Moderate (narrative review) | Mujika & Padilla 2000 Part I, Sports Med 30(2):79-87, PMID 10966148, doi:10.2165/00007256-200030020-00002 |

## Mechanism

### Strength outlives size

This is the single most useful finding for setting restart loads: muscle __size__ and muscle __strength__ decay on different clocks. Fibre cross-sectional area reverses inside 4 weeks (Mujika Part I) and had returned fully to baseline after 20 weeks of detraining in Psilander's human trial - yet strength in that same trial stayed about 60% above pre-training. Mujika Part II reports the same asymmetry for strength athletes past 4 weeks: fibre areas significantly reduced, force production declining only slowly and often staying above untrained values for very long periods.

The retained component is largely neural and motor-learning: the skill of the lift persists longer than the tissue that performs it.

Practical consequence: after a month or two off you will probably look smaller and feel weaker than you actually are, and the first sessions back usually go better than expected. That argues for a __modest__ restart cut and for re-testing rather than assuming, not for a dramatic rebuild from scratch.

### "Muscle memory" is better supported in mice than in men

Bruusgaard 2010 is a clean rodent mechanism: myonuclei acquired during overload persist through severe atrophy, apparently shielded from the apoptotic activity in inactive muscle, and a prior hypertrophy episode retarded later disuse atrophy. It is a genuinely plausible substrate for faster regrowth in previously trained tissue.

But when Psilander 2019 tested that prediction directly in humans - one previously trained leg against one untrained leg, both then retrained - the trained leg did not respond better, and myonuclear number did not change in either. So the mechanism has strong animal support and the human performance prediction failed in the trial designed to test it. Do not promise faster regain. Expect __retained strength__ instead, which is the better-supported and more useful finding anyway.

### Breaks cost less than they feel like they cost

Ogasawara 2013 is the most reassuring datum here: 3-weeks-off / 6-weeks-on cycles matched 24 weeks of continuous training on both CSA and 1RM. The caveats matter - 14 young men, bench press only, and the equality partly reflects continuous training's own tapering rate of gain rather than detraining being free of cost. But it does mean a 3-week travel gap is not a catastrophe, and it does not warrant restarting a lift from far below where it was.

### Ramping back: what is and is not established

Gabbett's review supports the general shape of the caution - large, rapid load increases track with non-contact soft-tissue injury, and a well-developed chronic load base is protective rather than dangerous. That much is worth respecting when coming off a layoff, because the downside of a spike is precisely the kind of injury this program is built to avoid.

What is __not__ supportable is the specific tool that came out of that literature. Impellizzeri 2020 dismantled the acute:chronic workload ratio: no study has properly estimated a causal effect, the metric mishandles ratio data, it is ambiguous, and it is not consistently or unidirectionally related to injury risk. Their conclusion is blunt - there is no evidence supporting its use. So ramp deliberately on general grounds, but do not cite a ratio threshold as though it were a validated rule.

## Practical Application (calibrated for Menotti)

### Restart-load bands

Referenced to the last __clean__ working set - full prescribed reps at target RPE - not to the last session logged, which may have been a reduced power or contrast day.

| Time off the lift | Restart load | Expected rebuild |
|---|---|---|
| Under 2 weeks | Resume at the last clean load | Normal double progression |
| 2-4 weeks | 5-10% under | Back to prior load in 1-2 sessions |
| 4-8 weeks | 10-15% under | Back to prior load in 2-4 sessions |
| Over 8 weeks, or any injury layoff affecting the loaded tissue | 15-20% under, and re-test rather than assume | 3-6 sessions, gated on symptom reads |

__These bands are a judgment call, not a published protocol.__ No trial prescribes restart percentages after a layoff. They are reasoned from the direction and dose-dependence of the Bosquet effect sizes, the strength-outlives-size asymmetry, and the general load-spike caution. They are deliberately biased __small__, because the evidence says strength is retained better than it feels. This is the weakest claim in the file and should be revised against Menotti's own data as layoffs accumulate.

### Rules that follow from the evidence

- __Reference the last clean set, not the last logged one.__ In Jul 2026 the last logged trap bar deadlift was 4x3 at 245 on 6/18, a reduced contrast session; the last clean 4x5 at 245 was 6/11. The clean set is the anchor.
- __Cut load, keep the movement.__ Per Mujika Part II, adaptations are preserved by maintaining intensity while cutting volume markedly and frequency only moderately. On a travel week, prefer a few hard sets of the real lifts at near-normal load over a lot of easy bodyweight volume.
- __Expect a fast climb, then normal progression.__ Two clean sessions earn the standard increment (+10 lb lower, +5 lb upper; no fractional plates are available, see CLAUDE.md Equipment Notes). Do not slow-roll a rebuild that is going well.
- __Do not add a deload to the far side of a layoff.__ The layoff already deloaded you. Prophylactic deloading in non-fatigued lifters costs strength (Coleman 2024, see [in-season-volume.md](in-season-volume.md)), and stacking one after a detraining stretch compounds the loss.
- __Separate the deconditioned tissue from the injured tissue.__ After an injury layoff there are two different reasons to reduce load, and they call for different amounts. Barbell lower returning after a calf strain is mostly a __deconditioning__ problem, not a calf problem: the trap bar deadlift is hip-dominant, and the squat's bottom position has the knee flexed, which slackens the biarticular gastroc rather than stretching it. See [calf-strain-rehab.md](calf-strain-rehab.md) for which movements actually load a healing gastroc - the answer is plyometrics, sprinting, and change of direction, not the barbell lifts.
- __Log the morning-after read on the first two or three sessions back.__ Cheapest early-warning signal available, and the one most often skipped.
- __On travel, the goal is to limit the gap, not replicate the program.__ Even minimal hard loading keeps the chronic base up, which is the protective variable in Gabbett 2016.

### Age note

Bosquet found greater losses in adults over 65. At 40 Menotti is not in that band, so do not import the elderly detraining literature or its urgency. The relevant age adjustment remains the roughly 10-15% lower recovery capacity already built into the program's 3-day lifting schedule and conservative volume (Lexell 1995, see CLAUDE.md Section 7).

## Unresolved Questions

- __No trial prescribes restart loads after a layoff.__ The band table above is reasoned, not measured. Treat it as a starting heuristic to be corrected by Menotti's own logged results.
- __Human muscle memory is genuinely unresolved.__ Strong rodent mechanism (Bruusgaard 2010) against a negative human performance test (Psilander 2019, n=19, single trial). Do not assert it in either direction.
- __Detraining data in 40-year-old trained lifters is thin.__ Bosquet stratified around age 65, Ogasawara used young men, Psilander used young adults. Extrapolation to a trained 40-year-old is unflagged in the primary literature and is an assumption, not a finding.
- __How much sport offsets lower-body detraining is unknown.__ Menotti's layoffs are rarely total, and he often keeps playing games. No literature converts basketball or hockey exposure into resistance-training equivalence; [in-season-volume.md](in-season-volume.md) already establishes that game-load-to-lifting-set equivalence is not established.
- __Nothing here says upper and lower body decay at different rates.__ The upper line survived the Jun-Jul 2026 layoff because it kept being trained. That is a training-frequency effect, not evidence of differential retention.
