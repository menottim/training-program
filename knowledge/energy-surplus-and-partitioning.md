# Energy Surplus and Nutrient Partitioning: What Fraction of Gained Weight Is Muscle

## Scope

This file covers one question. When body weight rises, how much of the gain should be lean mass and how much should be fat?

It covers four topics. First, the lean-to-fat split that overfeeding produces without resistance training. Second, what resistance training and protein change about that split. Third, the recommended rate of weight gain. Fourth, how to judge a real measured split against those benchmarks.

This file does not prescribe calorie targets, food choices or meal plans. Intake management is out of scope for this program, and the athlete holds that lever. See the Overview in `CLAUDE.md`.

__Origin.__ This file was written on 2026-08-24. The athlete asked whether his measured lean-mass and fat-mass changes were normal for strength training, or whether the fat gain was outpacing what a calorie surplus would explain. No existing `knowledge/` file covered partitioning.

## Mechanism (what the controlled data actually shows)

### The surplus sets the fat gain. Training and protein set the lean gain.

Bray et al. (2012, JAMA 307(1):47-55) is the cleanest separation of these two levers. 25 adults lived in a metabolic unit for 10 to 12 weeks. They were overfed by approximately 954 kcal per day, which is approximately 40% above their stabilization intake, for the final 8 weeks. The only variable was protein: 5%, 15% or 25% of energy.

Total weight gain differed by group. It was 3.16 kg on low protein, 6.05 kg on normal protein and 6.51 kg on high protein. __Fat gain did not differ.__ It rose similarly in all three groups. The extra weight in the higher-protein groups was lean tissue: no rise in body protein on 5%, a rise of 2.87 kg on 15% and 3.18 kg on 25%.

The authors state the conclusion directly. Calories alone account for the increase in fat.

The practical reading is blunt. You cannot train or eat protein your way out of the fat that a surplus deposits. Training and protein decide whether you also add muscle. They do not cancel the fat.

### Without training, the split is approximately 1 lean to 2 fat

Bouchard et al. (1990, NEJM 322(21):1477-82) overfed 12 pairs of young adult male identical twins by 1,000 kcal per day, 6 days per week, for 84 days inside a 100-day period. The total surplus was 353 MJ, which is approximately 84,000 kcal. The men stayed sedentary.

Mean weight gain was 8.1 kg, with a range of 4.3 to 13.3 kg. The mean composition was 5.4 kg fat and 2.7 kg fat-free mass. __That is 33% lean and 67% fat.__

Within-pair similarity was high, and between-pair variance was approximately 3 times the within-pair variance. Genetics explain a large share of who gains fat easily.

__This 33% figure is the benchmark that matters most for judging a real result.__ It is what a surplus does with no training at all. A training athlete should beat it. An athlete who matches it is getting no partitioning benefit from the training.

### A surplus adds fat even under expert guidance

Garthe et al. (2013, Eur J Sport Sci 13(3):295-303) randomized 39 elite athletes to nutritional counseling or ad libitum eating, across 8 to 12 weeks, with 4 added strength sessions per week.

The counseled group ate 3,585 kcal per day and gained 3.9% of body weight, with a 15% rise in fat mass. The ad libitum group ate 2,964 kcal and gained 1.5% of body weight, with a 3% rise in fat mass. Strength rose 6 to 12% in both groups, with no difference between them.

The authors caution against excess intake in a weight-gain protocol, because of the undesirable rise in body fat. Young elite athletes on 4 strength sessions per week still added fat in proportion to the surplus.

### Muscle is cheap to build. Fat is expensive to store.

Slater et al. (2019, Front Nutr 6:131) collects the deposition-cost estimates. Adding 1 kg of muscle costs approximately 6,050 to 7,440 kJ, which is approximately 1,450 to 1,780 kcal. Adding 1 kg of fat costs approximately 7,700 kcal.

Muscle is approximately 75% water and approximately 20% protein, so the tissue itself stores little energy. Fat stores approximately 9.4 kcal per gram of lipid.

The asymmetry drives the practical recommendation. A muscle gain of 0.5 kg per month needs only approximately 25 kcal per day of extra energy for the tissue itself. Anything much above a few hundred kcal per day is funding fat, not muscle. Slater et al. recommend starting at approximately 360 to 480 kcal per day and reviewing body composition, rather than starting high.

The upkeep cost of new muscle is also minor. Skeletal muscle burns approximately 54 kJ per kg per day, so 1 to 2 kg of new muscle raises resting expenditure by approximately 100 kJ. That is inside measurement error. New muscle does not meaningfully raise metabolic rate.

## Practical Application

1. __Judge the split, not just the weight.__ Compute fat mass and lean mass at each end of the window. Report the lean share of the total gain. A rising weight with a rising lean share is working. A rising weight at or below a 33% lean share is a surplus with no partitioning benefit.

2. __Use these benchmarks for the lean share of a weight gain:__

   | Lean share of the gain | Reading |
   |---|---|
   | Above approximately 50% | Good. Training and intake are both working. |
   | Approximately 33 to 40% | No better than sedentary overfeeding. Training is not shifting the split. |
   | Below approximately 33% | Worse than sedentary overfeeding. Look for a training gap in the window. |

3. __Check the rate separately from the split.__ Target approximately 0.25 to 0.5% of body weight per week, and stay under that as training age rises. A rate inside the range with a bad split is a composition problem, not a speed problem. The two failures need different responses.

4. __Estimate the implied surplus before calling it a diet failure.__ Multiply the fat gain in lb by approximately 3,500 kcal. Add the lean gain in lb multiplied by approximately 700 kcal. Divide by the days in the window. A surplus of 100 to 300 kcal per day is a small drift, not overeating. Naming the size prevents an overreaction to a number that looks alarming in total.

5. __Distrust a lean-mass gain that appears during a training gap.__ Detraining returns muscle thickness and cross-sectional area toward baseline (Psilander et al. 2019, PMID 30991013, in `detraining-and-return.md`). A consumer BIA scale does not measure lean mass. It estimates total body water and then derives fat-free mass from an equation (`body-composition-measurement.md`). Glycogen and water therefore read as lean mass. A lean gain across weeks with no training is most likely water, and the true fat gain is then larger than reported.

6. __Do not attribute a fat gain to the training.__ Bray 2012 settles this. The surplus deposits the fat. Training decides whether muscle comes with it. A report on a fat gain should name the energy balance, not the program.

7. __Stay inside scope.__ This program does not manage intake and does not state calorie or protein targets. Describe what the measured split implies, and leave the intake decision to the athlete. Descriptive analysis is in scope. A prescription is not.

## Unresolved or Bleeding-Edge Questions

- __The optimal surplus for a trained lifter is unknown.__ Slater et al. 2019 state this explicitly. No study has established the surplus that maximizes muscle relative to fat in trained individuals. Every number above comes from either untrained overfeeding or short athlete trials.
- __Partitioning in a masters athlete.__ The overfeeding data is from men aged approximately 18 to 35. Whether the 33% benchmark shifts at age 40 and above is untested.
- __Recomposition ceilings.__ Simultaneous fat loss and muscle gain is documented, but mostly in untrained, overweight or obese people. How much a trained intermediate can recompose at a maintenance intake is not settled.
- __Individual variance is large and partly genetic.__ Bouchard 1990 found approximately 3 times more between-pair than within-pair variance. Group means may predict an individual poorly.
- __Consumer BIA cannot resolve a partitioning question.__ The lean-mass figure is derived, not measured. A partitioning analysis that needs 1 to 2 lb of resolution needs DEXA under a strict protocol.
