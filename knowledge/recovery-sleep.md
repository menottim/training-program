# Sleep, Recovery, and Fatigue Auto-Regulation

## Scope

This file covers five topics. First, the role of sleep in athletic adaptation. Second, the auto-regulation signals: RPE drift, HRV, and subjective wellness. Third, the evidence ranking for the common recovery methods: cold water immersion, massage, foam rolling, stretching, sauna, active recovery, compression, and contrast therapy. Fourth, the role of nutrition in recovery, which covers protein distribution and the effect of alcohol. Fifth, the red flags for overtraining.

The file is calibrated for a 40-year-old recreational athlete who trains approximately 6 hours per week, across lifting, basketball and hockey, and who has bilateral Achilles tendinopathy.

This file does not cover clinical sleep disorders, such as obstructive sleep apnea or chronic insomnia. It does not cover clinical fatigue pathology.

## Mechanism (what the controlled data actually shows)

### Sleep gives the largest return of any recovery method

Growth hormone pulses peak during slow-wave sleep, which is stage N3. The glymphatic system also clears metabolic waste during that stage. REM sleep consolidates motor learning, which matters in a skill-based sport. Chronic sleep restriction reduces glucose tolerance, raises cortisol, and shifts the autonomic balance toward sympathetic dominance. All three add to the recovery debt.

Mah et al. (2011, Sleep 34(7):943-50) extended sleep to approximately 10 hours in bed, for 5 to 7 weeks, in 11 male Division 1 basketball players. The 282-foot sprint time improved by approximately 0.7 seconds. Free-throw accuracy and three-point accuracy each increased by approximately 9%. Subjective physical and mental well-being also improved. The sample is small and specific. The effect size is large.

Knowles et al. (2018, J Sci Med Sport 21(9):959-68) reviewed 17 studies on acute sleep deprivation and strength. The pattern is consistent: __multi-joint compound lifts such as the squat, the deadlift and the bench press decline more than isolation work does__. Motivation and central drive appear to mediate the effect. Peripheral contractile function stays largely intact. What fails is the athlete's willingness or ability to recruit maximal effort.

Walsh et al. (2021, Br J Sports Med 55(7):356-68) is an IOC expert consensus. It argues directly against one sleep prescription for all athletes. Individual sleep need varies genetically, and it may increase during a heavy training block. The 7 to 9 hour range (Hirshkowitz et al. 2015 NSF consensus) is the floor for general health. An athlete in an active training block often needs the upper end of that range, or more.

### The evidence on recovery methods is clearer than most people think

Dupuy et al. (2018, Front Physiol 9:403) meta-analyzed recovery interventions against each other. A clear hierarchy emerged for reducing DOMS, fatigue and the biomarkers of muscle damage. Massage came first. Active recovery, compression garments and cold water immersion followed, at approximately equal effect. Stretching produced a null result.

Guo et al. (2017, Front Physiol 8:747) meta-analyzed massage specifically and confirmed a large effect on DOMS. Wiewelhove et al. (2019, Front Physiol 10:376) meta-analyzed foam rolling. It gives small improvements in range of motion and a modest reduction in DOMS. Its effects on performance are marginal.

Cold water immersion carries an important caveat. Leeder et al. (2012, Br J Sports Med 46(4):233-40) confirmed its short-term benefit for soreness. Roberts et al. (2015, J Physiol 593(18):4285-301) showed that regular cold water immersion after lifting __reduces satellite cell activity and mTOR signaling__, and that it substantially reduced the hypertrophy gains over 12 weeks.

The practical rule follows. Cold water immersion is useful in season, when the priority is to perform in tomorrow's game. It works against you during a strength or hypertrophy block. Do not use an ice bath routinely after lifting if growth is the goal.

Static stretching needs a specific correction. Small et al. (2008, Res Sports Med 16(3):213-31) reviewed static stretching before exercise for injury prevention. They found __no protective effect__. A static stretch held longer than 60 seconds before exercise can reduce maximal force and power output by 3 to 5% for a short period. The Cochrane review by Herbert and de Noronha examined stretching for DOMS. It found an average reduction of approximately 1 point on a 100-point scale, which is clinically meaningless.

### Overtraining is a continuum, not a single state

Meeusen et al. (2013, MSSE 45(1):186-205; Eur J Sport Sci 13(1):1-24) is a joint ECSS and ACSM consensus. It describes a continuum with three stages:

1. __Functional overreaching.__ A short-term decrement, followed by supercompensation. This state is desirable.
2. __Non-functional overreaching.__ Weeks of stagnation. A deload can recover it.
3. __Overtraining syndrome.__ Months of impairment. It often needs complete rest and clinical intervention.

No single biomarker reliably diagnoses overtraining syndrome. The characteristic signs usually appear together: a performance decrement, mood disturbance, disrupted sleep, an elevated resting heart rate, and reduced HRV.

Plews et al. (2013, Sports Med 43(9):773-81; 2014, Int J Sports Physiol Perform 9(5):783-90) reviewed HRV as an auto-regulation tool. A single-day reading is too noisy, because sleep, alcohol, caffeine, stress and measurement variance all affect it. A 7-day rolling average with at least 3 readings per week is the minimum for useful trending. For a recreational athlete, use HRV as a two-way signal to push or to back off. Do not use it to prescribe an exact training load.

### Alcohol carries a large and specific cost

Parr et al. (2014, PLoS ONE 9(2):e88384) gave resistance-trained men alcohol after exercise, at 1.5 g per kg of body weight. For a 220 lb adult that is approximately 12 standard drinks. The crossover design tested the alcohol with and without protein taken at the same time.

Alcohol suppressed post-exercise myofibrillar protein synthesis by approximately 24%, even with protein taken at the same time. It suppressed it by approximately 37% when carbohydrate was taken instead of protein.

The dose is high, and it is unrealistic for typical social drinking. The mechanism, which is disruption of mTOR signaling, does depend on the dose. Even a moderate intake of 3 to 4 drinks disrupts sleep architecture, because it suppresses REM sleep in the second half of the night.

## Practical Application

1. __Target 7.5 to 9 hours in bed each night, with consistent bed and wake times.__ Keep both times inside a 30-minute window, including at weekends. For a Monday to Friday, 9 to 5 schedule, lights out at 10:30 or 11 PM with a 7 AM wake time is a reasonable anchor. The Walsh 2021 IOC consensus supports adjusting this to the athlete's own need. Consider a 20-minute nap on a heavy training day. Do not nap longer than 30 minutes, because that causes sleep inertia.

2. __Auto-regulate the lifts with RPE and readiness heuristics. Do not use a gadget HRV reading.__ On the day after basketball or hockey, check two signals. If the warm-up sets feel slow, or the first working set is 1 or more RPE grades above expectation, reduce the top-set weight by 10 to 15%, or reduce the volume. Knowles 2018 shows that a sleep-poor day affects compound lifts specifically. On such a day, you can replace the squat or the deadlift variant with isolation work. Single-joint strength survives sleep debt largely intact.

3. __Protein distribution matters more than precise timing__ (Aragon and Schoenfeld 2013). Target 1.6 to 2.2 g per kg per day, which is 160 to 220 g for a 220 lb athlete. Spread it across 4 meals at approximately 0.4 g per kg each. Protein inside a few hours after the session is sufficient. The 30-minute anabolic window is folklore. See `protein-distribution.md` for the detailed dosing.

4. __Use cold water immersion selectively. Do not use it routinely.__
   - __Use it__ after a game, when the next performance falls inside 48 hours and readiness matters more than adaptation.
   - __Do not use it__ after a lifting session during a strength or hypertrophy block. Roberts 2015 shows that regular cold water immersion after lifting reduces muscle growth.
   - __Consider__ a contrast shower, or a cool-only bath at approximately 12 to 15 degrees C for 10 minutes, after a game. Both are less extreme, and both are less likely to suppress the anabolic signal.

5. __Recovery methods, in order of return for the time spent:__
   1. Longer and more consistent sleep. This is free, and it has the strongest evidence.
   2. Zone 2 active recovery, as a walk or an easy bike ride, 20 to 30 minutes on an off day. This helps fatigue clearance and adds no damage.
   3. Massage, or a percussion gun, on a sore muscle group. This has the largest DOMS effect size, per Dupuy 2018.
   4. Foam rolling. Use it before a session as a warm-up, and after a session to maintain range of motion. The effect is small but real.
   5. Sauna, 2 to 3 times per week, 15 to 20 minutes, on a non-training day. Scoon 2007 shows plasma volume benefits.
   6. Static stretching. It is acceptable for a range-of-motion goal. It does not prevent injury. Do not do it before heavy lifting or explosive play.

6. __Alcohol has a real cost.__ Limit it to 1 or 2 drinks on a training day. Do not drink inside 3 hours of bed. The 12-drink experiment in Parr 2014 is unrealistic, but the mechanism depends on the dose. Even 3 to 4 drinks disrupt REM sleep in the second half of the night, which adds to the next day's recruitment deficit.

7. __Overtraining red flags. Three or more of these justify a 7 to 10 day deload. Do not train through them.__
   - A performance plateau or decrement that lasts more than 2 weeks, despite continued training.
   - Resting heart rate more than 5 bpm above his normal value, for 3 or more consecutive days.
   - Persistent morning mood disturbance, such as irritability or a lack of enthusiasm.
   - Weight loss with no explanation.
   - Disrupted sleep, despite adequate opportunity to sleep.
   - Frequent minor illnesses, or cuts that heal slowly.
   - Achilles pain that gets worse at rest, not only during activity.

## Unresolved or Bleeding-Edge Questions

- __HRV-guided training for a recreational athlete.__ Most of the research used elite endurance athletes. The 7-day rolling-average heuristic (Plews 2014) is defensible. No one has validated the exact load-adjustment thresholds for a middle-aged mixed-sport athlete.
- __The accuracy of sleep stages on a consumer wearable.__ Total sleep time detection is reasonable, at approximately 85 to 90% against polysomnography. REM and deep-stage classification reaches only 60 to 79% at best (Chinoy 2021; Berryhill 2020). Treat the "deep sleep minutes" metric as noise. Use total sleep time and consistency instead.
- __Sauna as a recovery method against sauna as an adaptation stimulus.__ Scoon 2007 and Stanley 2015 suggest cardiovascular and plasma-volume benefits. Heat exposure activates heat shock proteins and mTOR-independent pathways. Those may add to the training adaptations, or interfere with them. No one has established the best placement, whether morning, after training, or on a rest day.
- __Peptide and supplement claims for tendon recovery, such as BPC-157 and TB-500.__ There are no human RCTs. The veterinary and rodent data is preliminary. Do not recommend them.
- __Mouth taping, grounding or earthing, and infrared mats.__ No quality evidence supports a recovery benefit.

## Corrections Log

- __2026-04-18:__ This file was created. It corrects seven claims from gym culture and from earlier versions of CLAUDE.md:
  - __The "30 to 60 minute anabolic window" is refuted.__ Aragon and Schoenfeld 2013 show the window is approximately 4 to 6 hours wide in a fed athlete. Total daily protein and its distribution matter far more than narrow timing after the workout.
  - __The claim that static stretching prevents injury is refuted.__ The systematic reviews by Small 2008 and Herbert 2011 show no protective effect. A hold longer than 60 seconds may also reduce force and power for a short period.
  - __The claim that lactic acid causes DOMS is refuted.__ Blood lactate clears inside approximately 60 minutes after exercise. DOMS peaks 24 to 72 hours later. Mechanical microtrauma plus the inflammatory response cause it.
  - __The claim that an ice bath after every workout helps muscle growth is refuted.__ Roberts 2015 and the later meta-analyses show that regular cold water immersion after lifting __suppresses__ hypertrophy. Use cold water immersion for in-season game recovery. Do not use it as routine lifting recovery during a strength or hypertrophy block.
  - __The claim that stretching after exercise reduces soreness is refuted.__ The Cochrane review (Herbert and de Noronha 2011) found a negligible effect, at approximately 1 point on a 100-point scale.
  - __The claim that everyone needs exactly 8 hours of sleep is too simple.__ The NSF 2015 range is 7 to 9 hours. The Walsh 2021 IOC consensus recommends an individual target. An athlete in heavy training may need the upper end.
  - __The claim that a drop in HRV always means overtraining is too simple.__ Plews 2012 showed that HRV can fall during positive adaptation in some elite athletes. A 7-day rolling trend, combined with subjective wellness, is easier to interpret than a single reading.
