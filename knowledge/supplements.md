# Supplements: Athlete-Calibrated Evidence Review

## Scope

This file covers Menotti's current daily supplement stack, plus the alternatives people commonly ask about. Each item carries an evidence tier, and each is assessed against his specific profile: a 40-year-old male, approximately 222 lb, not hypertensive, with no documented nutrient deficiency, who plays basketball and hockey, lifts 3 times per week, and has bilateral Achilles tendinopathy.

This file does not cover clinical supplementation for a diagnosed deficiency. That needs a physician's guidance, with laboratory monitoring.

## Athlete Context

Menotti is a __regular Double Reds donor, also called Power Red. He donates 2 to 3 times per year.__

This context is critical for the iron review below. Each Power Red donation removes approximately 470 mg of iron, against 220 to 250 mg for whole blood. His annual iron loss from donation is therefore 940 to 1,410 mg. That makes him a clinically recognized frequent donor, who benefits from chronic iron supplementation, per the Red Cross and AABB guidance.

## Key Findings

### 1. Iron supplementation IS appropriate for Menotti, because he is a Double Reds donor

__This depends on context.__ The default guidance for a healthy non-deficient male is not to supplement iron without laboratory tests (Peeling and Zoller 2004, PMID 15212743). __Menotti is a regular Double Reds donor__, and that changes the calculation completely. Frequent donation is a clinically recognized indication for chronic iron supplementation.

__The iron arithmetic for Double Reds:__

- Each Power Red donation removes __approximately 470 mg of iron__. A standard whole blood donation removes 220 to 250 mg. Source: American Red Cross donor education materials.
- Menotti donates 2 to 3 times per year. That is __940 to 1,410 mg of iron lost each year from donation alone__.
- The body needs 24 to 30 weeks to fully replace its iron stores after a Power Red. That exceeds the 112-day minimum interval between donations.

__Consensus guidance for a frequent donor:__

- __The American Red Cross, in its 2020 to 2023 donor materials__, recommends 18 to 38 mg of elemental iron for 120 days after each Power Red donation. A multivitamin with 18 mg of iron is an alternative.
- __AABB Association Bulletin #17-02__, from 2017 and updated in 2022, gives the industry consensus. It recommends iron supplementation for a frequent donor, defined as a male who donates 3 or more times per year. It supports three interventions for donor iron deficiency: education, management based on ferritin testing, and supplementation.
- __The RISE study (Cable et al. 2011, PMID 22023513)__ followed 2,425 donors prospectively. __47% of frequent male donors had iron deficiency without supplementation__, and 18% had no iron stores at all. Supplementation reduces both figures substantially.
- __The STRIDE trial (Mast et al.)__ tested 19 mg and 38 mg of elemental iron daily for 60 days. Both doses mitigated donation-induced iron deficiency. Supplementation worked better than extending the interval between donations.

__Dose calibration:__

- The typical Red Cross recommendation is 18 to 38 mg of elemental iron.
- Menotti's current dose is 65 mg of elemental iron daily. That is __slightly above the official recommendation__. It is also the strength of a typical over-the-counter ferrous sulfate tablet: 325 mg of ferrous sulfate supplies 65 mg of elemental iron. He donates 2 to 3 times per year, not once, so the higher dose is defensible.
- His 125 mg of vitamin C, taken at the same time, approximately doubles non-heme iron absorption. That pairing is well designed.

__The practical recommendation, at evidence tier Strong: continue the iron supplementation.__ The 65 mg dose is in the correct range for a regular Power Red donor. Stopping it would create a predictable iron deficit.

__Annual monitoring is still worthwhile:__

- Request __serum ferritin, hemoglobin, hematocrit, MCV and transferrin saturation__ once a year. Get it at a blood-donation center, or at the annual physical.
- The ferritin target is 30 to 200 ng/mL. A value below 30 indicates deficiency despite the supplement, so consider a higher dose or fewer donations. A value above 200 indicates the supplement may exceed his need, so consider dropping to the 38 mg range. A consistent pattern of 200 to 500 ng/mL, especially with fatigue or joint symptoms, justifies an HFE gene test, given the bilateral tendinopathy.
- Many Red Cross and Vitalant centers now offer ferritin testing at donation. Ask at the next appointment.

### 2. Fish oil at 980 mg of EPA plus DHA is probably too low for an athletic effect

The evidence for omega-3 in athletes is Moderate. It supports doses meaningfully higher than his current 980 mg of EPA plus DHA.

- __Muscle recovery and DOMS__ (Jouris et al. 2011; Tsuchiya et al. 2020; several RCTs). Effective doses for reducing post-exercise muscle damage markers and perceived soreness run from __2,000 to 4,200 mg of EPA plus DHA daily__. Most studies use a minimum of 2 to 3 g per day, for 4 weeks or more.
- __Tendon healing__ (Irani et al. 2025, Am J Sports Med, PMID 40376939). Omega-3 plus exercise improved Achilles tendon collagen alignment and cell arrangement in an animal model. The human clinical data is Emerging.
- __Cardiovascular and triglyceride benefits__ (AHA guidelines). Use 1 g per day of EPA plus DHA for general cardiovascular prevention. Use 2 to 4 g per day to lower triglycerides.
- __The upper safe limit.__ The FDA generally recognizes up to 3 g per day of EPA plus DHA as safe for general consumption. The European EFSA accepts up to 5 g per day.

__The practical recommendation, at evidence tier Moderate.__ His current 980 mg dose sits at the low end for a general cardiovascular benefit. It is __probably below the threshold for a muscle-recovery or tendon-specific effect__. Doubling it to approximately 2 g of EPA plus DHA daily is reasonable, if tendon and recovery support is a priority. Read the label for the total EPA plus DHA content, not the total fish oil in mg. Aim for 2,000 to 3,000 mg of EPA plus DHA.

### 3. Magnesium glycinate at 240 mg is well chosen

The evidence supports both the dose and the form.

- __The form__ (Mayo Clinic Press review 2023; Banner Health). Magnesium glycinate is easier on the gut than magnesium oxide or magnesium citrate. It causes fewer loose stools.
- __The dose.__ The typical supplemental range is 200 to 400 mg of elemental magnesium. 240 mg sits comfortably inside that range.
- __Sleep quality, evidence tier Moderate.__ Several small RCTs show a modest improvement in sleep quality, in older adults and in magnesium-deficient populations. The effect is smaller in a non-deficient adult.
- __Muscle cramps, evidence tier Weak.__ Cochrane and other systematic reviews find no robust evidence that magnesium prevents or treats an exercise-associated muscle cramp. It may help if dietary magnesium is low. It is not a general anti-cramp intervention.

__The practical recommendation:__ keep it. If he takes it near bedtime, the glycine may add a modest sleep benefit. This is not an important decision either way.

### 4. Creatine monohydrate at 5 g per day: the gold standard. Keep it unchanged

Creatine is __the most studied ergogenic supplement in sports nutrition__. It has the strongest evidence base of anything in Menotti's stack.

__The primary source__ is the [Kreider et al. 2017 ISSN Position Stand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28615996/) (J Int Soc Sports Nutr 14:18). It covers safety, efficacy and therapeutic applications.

__Effectiveness, evidence tier Strong:__

- Kreider 2017 calls it __"the most effective ergogenic nutritional supplement currently available to athletes with the intent of increasing high-intensity exercise capacity and lean body mass during training"__. That is a direct quote.
- Meta-analyses show approximately 8% more strength and approximately 14% more high-intensity work capacity, against a placebo.
- The mechanism: creatine increases muscle phosphocreatine stores, which supports ATP regeneration during an explosive or high-intensity effort. Examples: a basketball sprint, a lift, and a hockey burst.
- The improvements continue with continued use. No tolerance develops.

__Safety, evidence tier Strong:__

- Kreider 2017 states: __"Short and long-term supplementation (up to 30 g/day for 5 years) is safe and well-tolerated in healthy individuals."__
- It has no effect on kidney function in a healthy adult, despite persistent internet folklore.
- Three myths are refuted. There is no need to cycle off it. Most studies show no evidence of hair loss. There is no dehydration risk.

__Dosing, evidence tier Strong:__

- The standard maintenance dose is __3 to 5 g per day__. It saturates the muscle in 3 to 4 weeks.
- A loading phase is optional: 20 g per day for 5 to 7 days, then 5 g per day as maintenance. Loading saturates the muscle faster. It is not necessary.
- 5 g per day is the standard maintenance dose for an adult of Menotti's size. No adjustment is needed.

__Benefits in an older adult, evidence tier Strong.__ The Chilibeck et al. 2017 meta-analysis, and later work, show that creatine plus resistance training in an adult of 55 or older improves lean mass, strength, functional capacity and bone health, beyond exercise alone. Functional capacity includes the timed-up-and-go test and the sit-to-stand test. Menotti is 40, so he does not fall in that cohort. The benefits still point in a supportive direction.

__Emerging areas, evidence tier Moderate to Emerging:__

- __Cognitive function under sleep deprivation.__ Gordji-Nejad et al. 2024 (Sci Rep) showed that a single high dose of creatine improved cognition in sleep-deprived adults. This applies to a game day after poor sleep.
- __Tendon health.__ There is mechanistic evidence that intracellular hydration and ATP availability may support collagen synthesis under load. No human tendinopathy RCT exists yet. Do not claim an Achilles benefit. It is a plausible minor tailwind alongside HSR.
- __Bone mineral density.__ The evidence is growing, especially in older adults.

__What this means for Menotti:__

- 5 g per day in the shake is the correct dose. Change nothing.
- Taking it with carbohydrate mildly increases uptake, through insulin-mediated transport. His shake contains milk, yogurt and PB Fit. This is not required.
- Expect 1 to 3 lb of water retention, primarily __inside the cells__, as muscle cell hydration. This is adaptive. It is not fat. The extra cell water supports protein synthesis.
- No cycling is needed.

### 5. Psyllium husk powder at 5 g per day: supported at a modest dose. Increase it for the full LDL effect

Psyllium is a soluble fiber, from Plantago ovata. It has a strong evidence base across several areas.

- __LDL cholesterol reduction, evidence tier Strong.__ Jovanovski et al. 2018 (Am J Clin Nutr, PMID 30239559) reviewed and meta-analyzed 28 trials. A daily dose of 10 g of psyllium lowers LDL by approximately 13 mg/dL in hypercholesterolemic adults. The effect depends on the dose. A reduction of 5 to 10% in LDL is typical at 7 to 15 g per day. See the [Harvard Health psyllium summary](https://www.health.harvard.edu/heart-health/psyllium-fiber-regularity-and-healthier-lipid-levels).
- __The FDA qualified health claim__ requires 7 g of soluble fiber from psyllium per day, which is approximately 10 to 12 g of psyllium husk, to support the claim of a reduced cardiovascular-disease risk.
- __Glycemic control, evidence tier Moderate.__ Psyllium slows gastric emptying and glucose absorption. It reduces the blood glucose response after a meal. It is particularly useful around a high-carbohydrate meal.
- __Satiety, evidence tier Moderate.__ The soluble fiber expands in the gut, which reduces appetite and extends satiety. It is mildly useful on a deficit day.
- __Gut regularity, evidence tier Strong.__ It acts as a bulk-forming laxative, and the effect is well characterized.
- __Safety, evidence tier Strong.__ It is well tolerated at 5 to 15 g per day. The main side effects are bloating, gas and abdominal discomfort, early in use. Take it with plenty of water, to prevent a GI obstruction.

__What this means for Menotti.__ His 5 g per day in the shake is a baseline dose. At that level he gets the gut-regularity and mild satiety benefits. He is probably below the threshold for a meaningful LDL reduction. If a lipid panel shows a borderline LDL, an increase to 10 g per day would probably produce a measurable cardiovascular benefit. Split that dose across 2 shakes, or take one larger dose.

__The practical recommendation:__ keep 5 g for now. At the next annual physical, request a lipid panel alongside the ferritin check. If LDL is elevated, increase psyllium to 10 to 15 g per day, in split doses, for 8 to 12 weeks, then recheck.

### 6. One A Day Men's multivitamin: low value, and not harmful

- __Evidence tier Weak.__ ISSN and ACSM guidance agree that a multivitamin does not improve performance in an athlete with an adequate diet (Aragon et al. 2017 ISSN position stand). Where a benefit exists, it appears in three populations: people with a specific deficiency, athletes in a weight-controlled sport under energy restriction, and people on a restrictive diet.
- __One potential downside.__ Some multivitamin formulas contain iron, which would add to his separate iron supplement. __Read the label.__ The standard One A Day Men's Health Formula generally contains 0 mg of iron, unlike the women's formulas. Verify it on the bottle.
- __A caution about antioxidants.__ Recent consensus (Merry and Ristow 2016; ISSN 2017) warns that high-dose vitamin C and vitamin E, taken around a workout, can __reduce mitochondrial biogenesis and redox signaling__, which reduces the training adaptation. One A Day contains moderate doses: 60 to 90 mg of vitamin C and 30 IU of vitamin E. Those are unlikely to cause the effect. Be aware of it.

__The practical recommendation:__ neutral. If his diet already includes vegetables, fruit, lean protein and whole grains, the multivitamin is redundant. If the diet coverage is uncertain, it is cheap insurance. It is not harmful, once he verifies that it contains 0 mg of iron, which avoids compounding with the separate iron supplement.

## Commonly Asked Alternatives and Their Evidence

### Vitamin D

- __Evidence tier Strong.__ Vitamin D deficiency is very common in athletes, especially in an indoor-sport athlete and in an athlete who trains through winter. Correcting a deficiency improves VO2max in a deficient athlete, and it may support tendon health and immune function.
- __The recommendation: test, do not guess.__ Serum 25-OH vitamin D must be 30 ng/mL or above, which is 75 nmol/L. The ideal range is 40 to 60 ng/mL. If it is below that, supplement 1,000 to 5,000 IU per day, based on how deep the deficiency is. If it is above that, he needs no supplement.
- __What this means for Menotti.__ He lives in San Diego, at a latitude of approximately 32 degrees N, and he plays basketball outdoors year-round. He is probably adequate without a supplement. A single 25-OH-D test at the next physician visit is a cheap data point.

### Collagen peptides with vitamin C, for the Achilles specifically

- __Evidence tier Emerging.__ Shaw et al. 2017 (Am J Clin Nutr, PMID 27852613) gave 15 g of gelatin plus 50 mg of vitamin C, 60 minutes before exercise. Serum PINP, a collagen synthesis marker, doubled. Praet et al. 2019 (Nutrients, PMID 30609761) gave 5 g of TENDOFORTE, a GELITA bioactive collagen peptide, plus calf strengthening. VISA-A improved faster than with exercise alone, in Achilles tendinopathy, over 6 months.
- __What this means for Menotti.__ He has bilateral Achilles tendinopathy, which makes this an __Emerging-tier candidate worth a trial__. Timing: 30 to 60 minutes before HSR, or before basketball or hockey. Dose: 10 to 15 g of hydrolyzed collagen peptides, or 5 g if the product is specifically GELITA TENDOFORTE or FORTIGEL, plus approximately 50 mg of vitamin C. `knowledge/achilles-tendinopathy-hsr.md` also covers this.

__Dosing specifics:__

- Dose. With a GELITA product, either TENDOFORTE or FORTIGEL, 5 g is effective, per Praet 2019. With generic hydrolyzed collagen, aim for 10 to 15 g, which is the gelatin equivalent from Shaw 2017.
- Vitamin C. Pair approximately 50 mg with the collagen dose. His iron supplement contains 125 mg of vitamin C. If he takes the two together, that covers both needs.
- Duration. Shaw 2017 showed the collagen-synthesis marker doubling inside hours. Praet 2019 ran for 6 months. Jerger 2022 showed Achilles tendon adaptation at 12 weeks. Run the trial for at least 12 weeks before you judge the clinical effect.

__On timing: a pre-exercise dose is mechanism-optimized, and a daily dose at any time is clinically validated__

Two separate evidence bases exist for timing.

- __Pre-exercise, 30 to 60 minutes before loading. Strong evidence for an acute collagen synthesis marker response.__ Shaw 2017 gave 15 g of gelatin plus vitamin C, 60 minutes before 6 minutes of rope-skipping. Serum PINP, a collagen formation marker, doubled. The mechanism: amino acids peak at approximately 60 minutes after ingestion, which coincides with the loading window, when tendon blood flow briefly rises. The work by Lis and Baar at UC Davis on engineered ligaments supports this timing-sensitive model.
- __Daily chronic dosing, at any time. Moderate to Strong evidence for clinical outcomes.__ Praet 2019, an Achilles tendinopathy RCT, and Jerger 2022, a 12-week training study, both used a daily dose rather than a pre-exercise dose. Both produced clinical improvement. A 2024 systematic review (PMC11561013) on long-term collagen plus training concluded that __a daily dose of 15 g for 8 weeks or more__ drives tendon remodeling.

__No head-to-head RCT has compared pre-exercise dosing against daily dosing for a clinical outcome.__ Tendon adaptation is slow, at 8 to 12 weeks or more. Chronic substrate availability therefore probably matters more than acute timing, across the months that decide the clinical result.

__The practical order, balancing adherence against evidence:__

1. __The morning shake, daily. This is the recommendation for most cases.__ It gives the highest adherence, and it matches the clinical trial protocols in Praet 2019 and Jerger 2022. Whey does not dilute the effect of collagen. Their amino acid profiles complement each other: whey is rich in leucine, and collagen is rich in glycine, proline and hydroxyproline.
2. __At bedtime, daily.__ This is also clinically validated. Glycine has a mild sedative effect, and it pairs well with magnesium glycinate. There is a theoretical synergy with the growth hormone pulses of deep sleep, for connective-tissue remodeling.
3. __Pre-workout, 30 to 60 minutes before HSR or sport.__ This is mechanism-optimized, per Shaw 2017. It needs consistent workout timing. It only helps if the schedule allows a consistent pre-exercise dose, with no missed doses on a rest day.
4. __A split dose__, half in the morning and half pre-workout on a training day. This exceeds what the evidence supports. It is defensible if maximizing the theoretical mechanism matters to you.

__Adherence matters more than timing__, for a slow-adapting tissue on a 12 to 24 week trial. Missing a dose to chase perfect timing costs more than imperfect timing ever will.

__What to avoid:__

- __Choosing marine collagen over bovine collagen, or the reverse.__ Their efficacy is similar. Choose on preference or on allergens.
- __A product that advertises "collagen types I, II and III" as a selling point, with no GELITA ingredient and no NSF certification.__ That is a marketing signal, not a quality signal.
- __A collagen "elixir" with added sugar.__ That is a protein shake with decoration.

### Caffeine

- __Evidence tier Strong.__ The ISSN position stand (Guest et al. 2021) supports 3 to 6 mg per kg of body weight, which is approximately 300 to 600 mg for Menotti, taken 30 to 60 minutes before exercise. It improves endurance, power and alertness.
- __One caution.__ Chronic high-dose caffeine near bedtime disrupts sleep. Its half-life is approximately 5 to 6 hours. Limit intake after approximately 2 PM if sleep is a priority.

### Beta-alanine, citrulline malate, and beetroot

- __Beta-alanine.__ There is strong evidence for high-intensity exercise lasting 1 to 4 minutes (ISSN position stand). It is less relevant to basketball or hockey game performance, and more relevant to high-intensity intervals.
- __Citrulline malate, 6 to 8 g pre-workout.__ There is moderate evidence for volume-based resistance training performance. A 4-week trial is worthwhile if he is interested. The effect size is probably trivial for the current goals.
- __Beetroot juice, for its nitrates.__ The evidence is strong for endurance and moderate for team sports. 500 mL taken approximately 2 hours before a game may improve repeated-sprint performance. Treat it as experimental.

### Common stacks without solid evidence

- __BCAAs, the branched-chain amino acids.__ They are not worth the money for an athlete who reaches 1.6 to 2.2 g per kg of total protein. Total protein beats isolated BCAAs for muscle protein synthesis (Morton 2018).
- __Glutamine.__ There is minimal evidence for performance or recovery, outside clinical immune contexts.
- __Arginine and AAKG.__ The evidence is weak outside clinical cardiovascular populations.
- __Peptides, such as BPC-157 and TB-500.__ There are no human RCTs. Only veterinary and rodent data exists. Their legal status is ambiguous. Do not recommend them.
- __Prohormones, SARMs and TRT.__ These are out of scope for this program. If he considers them because of age-related symptoms, he needs comprehensive hormone panels, and he must work with a physician.

## Practical Application

1. __Continue the iron supplementation.__ It is clinically appropriate, given his Double Reds donor status at 2 to 3 donations per year. An __annual ferritin, hemoglobin and transferrin saturation__ check is still worthwhile, to confirm iron balance. Many blood donation centers now offer ferritin testing at donation.

2. __Consider increasing the fish oil to 2 to 3 g of EPA plus DHA daily__, if recovery and tendon support is a priority. Choose a bottle labeled by EPA plus DHA content, not by total fish oil.

3. __Confirm that One A Day Men's contains 0 mg of iron.__ If it contains any iron, that adds to the separate iron supplement, and it should change the iron-supplement decision.

4. __Consider adding collagen peptides with vitamin C before loading__, as an Emerging-evidence adjunct for the Achilles tendinopathy. Take 10 to 15 g of collagen peptides plus 50 mg of vitamin C, 30 to 60 minutes before HSR or sport.

5. __Test vitamin D at the next physician visit. Do not supplement blindly.__ He lives in sunny San Diego, so he is probably sufficient. A single test is a cheap data point.

6. __Keep the magnesium glycinate and the creatine unchanged.__ Both are well calibrated.

## Unresolved or Bleeding-Edge

- __Collagen for tendon clinical endpoints.__ The mechanism has strong support. The well-powered RCTs, with VISA-A or an equivalent outcome, are still small. The supplement is worth a trial. It is not yet consensus-tier.
- __The optimal omega-3 dose for a tendon outcome, against a muscle outcome.__ The range is wide, at 1.8 to 6 g per day, and the studies use inconsistent endpoints.
- __Antioxidants blunting adaptation around a workout.__ High doses of vitamin C and vitamin E near training appear to reduce the adaptation. Researchers are still characterizing the dose-response and the timing thresholds.
- __Individual iron needs vary.__ Three groups have a higher iron turnover: plant-based athletes, female athletes, and endurance athletes with repeated hemolysis from foot strike. Menotti's profile, a male who eats meat and plays mixed sports, carries a lower risk of iron loss than those groups.
