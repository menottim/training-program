# Protein: Daily Ceiling, Per-Meal Distribution, and Safety

## Scope

This file covers four topics: the optimal daily protein intake for a resistance-trained athlete, the per-meal anabolic window, the distribution strategies, and the safety of a high protein intake.

Use it to plan meals, to decide on supplementation, and to assess a day with uneven protein distribution.

The file is calibrated for a 40-year-old resistance-trained athlete. Its values are slightly above the RDA-level recommendations.

## Mechanism (what the controlled data actually shows)

### The daily ceiling is approximately 1.6 g per kg for muscle gain. Safety extends to 3.4 g per kg and above

Morton et al. (2018) ran a systematic review and meta-regression of 49 RCTs, with 1,863 participants. They examined how protein supplementation affected resistance-training outcomes. Gains in fat-free mass reached a plateau at __1.62 g of protein per kg per day__ (95% CI 1.03 to 2.20). Above that intake, more protein did not add muscle mass or strength.

The confidence interval is wide, from 1.03 to 2.20, so individual requirements vary. The upper bound of 2.2 g per kg is a reasonable target for an athlete who wants the full benefit without excess intake. The same meta-regression identified two moderators: increasing age reduces the effect, and resistance-training experience increases it.

Above the muscle-building ceiling, the body oxidizes the extra protein, or converts it to substrate through gluconeogenesis. That protein is not wasted in a nutritional sense. It supplies calories, and the thermic effect of food takes approximately 20 to 30% of protein calories (Westerterp 2004). It does not add muscle mass.

### The per-meal ceiling is approximately 0.4 g per kg. It is not a fixed 20 to 25 g

The older "muscle full" hypothesis stated that approximately 20 to 25 g of high-quality protein per meal maximally stimulates muscle protein synthesis, and that the body oxidizes anything above that. Schoenfeld and Aragon (2018) reviewed the hypothesis. They concluded that the ceiling scales with body size. It is not an absolute number.

Their recommendation is __0.4 g per kg of body weight per meal, across at least 4 meals, for a total of at least approximately 1.6 g per kg per day__. For a 100 kg athlete that is approximately 40 g per meal, and at least approximately 160 g per day. A larger dose in one meal can still add to anabolism, but the return diminishes above the 0.4 g per kg mark.

One important qualifier: the 0.4 g per kg ceiling comes from fast-absorbed protein sources such as whey. A slower protein, such as casein, or a whole-food mixed meal with fat and fiber, gives a longer absorption window. That may raise the effective per-meal ceiling, because it holds muscle protein synthesis elevated for longer.

### Distribution matters. Four meals beat two at the same total

Areta et al. (2013) tested three distribution patterns directly in young men, over 12 hours after resistance exercise. All three delivered the same 80 g total: 8 doses of 10 g, 4 doses of 20 g, and 2 doses of 40 g. __The 4 doses of 20 g produced significantly higher muscle protein synthesis__ than either the 2-dose pulse pattern or the 8-dose bleed pattern.

Mamerow et al. (2014) extended this to a 24-hour window in healthy adults. An even distribution of approximately 30 g per meal across 3 meals produced 25% higher 24-hour muscle protein synthesis than a skewed distribution. The skewed pattern gave approximately 10 g at breakfast, approximately 15 g at lunch, and approximately 65 g at dinner. Both patterns delivered identical daily totals. Applied to a typical "big dinner" eating pattern, this is direct evidence that moving protein earlier in the day works better than stacking it at night.

The size of the effect is modest, at 10 to 25% of daily muscle protein synthesis. Its consequence for muscle mass over months is subtle, not dramatic. Optimize the distribution only after the other variables are already handled: total intake, training and sleep.

### Older adults have a reduced per-meal MPS response

Moore et al. (2015) showed that an older adult needs a larger protein dose per meal to reach the same muscle protein synthesis response as a younger adult. The suggested per-meal dose was approximately 0.40 g per kg for an older adult, against approximately 0.24 g per kg for a younger adult. This is called anabolic resistance. Resistance training partly reverses it (Breen and Phillips 2013). Even so, an athlete of 40 or older should target the upper end of the 0.4 g per kg per-meal range, not the lower end.

### Safety: no evidence of harm up to 3.4 g per kg in a healthy adult

Antonio et al. (2016) put 14 resistance-trained men on a high-protein diet of approximately 3.3 to 3.4 g per kg per day, for one year. Kidney function, liver function and blood lipids did not change against their normal diet. A follow-up trial found that the same dose improved body composition when combined with heavy resistance training, and it produced no harmful markers.

A broader review (Antonio et al. 2020, Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition) confirms that a healthy adult with no pre-existing kidney disease tolerates 3.2 to 4.4 g per kg per day. The RDA of 0.8 g per kg is calibrated for minimum nitrogen balance in a sedentary adult. It underestimates the requirement for a training athlete.

One exception: a person with chronic kidney disease, or with reduced renal function, must consult a physician. The general safety conclusion applies to healthy adults only.

## Practical Application

1. __Target 1.6 to 2.2 g per kg of body weight per day.__ For a 100 kg athlete that is 160 to 220 g per day. Below 1.6 g per kg, the athlete probably leaves some muscle-building potential unused. Above 2.2 g per kg, there is no further muscle-building benefit. There is also no harm.

2. __Target 0.4 g per kg per meal, across 4 or more meals.__ For a 100 kg athlete that is four meals of approximately 40 g each. An athlete of 40 or older should aim closer to the top of this range per meal, to overcome mild anabolic resistance.

3. __Timing matters less than the total.__ The anabolic window immediately after a workout is largely a myth (Schoenfeld et al. 2013). The primary variable is the total daily intake, spread across meals. A protein meal inside a few hours before training covers the post-workout window adequately.

4. __Even distribution beats concentration at the same total.__ Suppose the current pattern is 0 g at breakfast, 30 g at lunch and 90 g at dinner. A move toward 30 g at each of 4 meals will probably produce more muscle protein synthesis from the same daily total. Do not sacrifice meal quality to chase a perfect distribution.

5. __Excess protein is calories first and muscle second.__ A day with 250 to 300 g of protein is not harmful. The muscle-building benefit plateaus at approximately 1.6 to 2.2 g per kg. The body oxidizes the extra 50 to 100 g for energy. Protein has a higher thermic cost than carbohydrate or fat: approximately 20 to 30%, against 5 to 10% for carbohydrate and 0 to 3% for fat (Westerterp 2004).

6. __A supplement is a tool. It is not a requirement.__ Whole-food protein works equally well. Whey, casein and mixed plant sources are convenient and cost-effective ways to reach a target. The ISSN position stand (Jäger et al. 2017) states that neither the source nor the timing changes the long-term outcome meaningfully, when the total intake is adequate.

## Unresolved or Bleeding-Edge Questions

- __The optimal per-meal dose in an older trained adult.__ The figure of approximately 0.4 g per kg comes mainly from studies in young adults. Moore et al. 2015 and Traylor et al. 2018 suggest that an older adult benefits from a larger per-meal dose, at approximately 0.6 g per kg. That data is less robust.
- __Plant protein against animal protein for muscle gain, at equal doses.__ Emerging evidence suggests that blended plant sources approach the effectiveness of animal sources when the total intake is adequate. No one has fully established the per-meal equivalence.
- __The leucine threshold as a practical rule.__ The idea that each meal needs approximately 2.5 to 3 g of leucine to trigger muscle protein synthesis has mechanistic support. No one has translated it clearly into whole-food meal planning, outside supplement design.
- __The role of protein in tendon remodeling.__ Direct evidence that protein supports tendinopathy recovery is emerging, particularly for collagen with vitamin C. It is not consensus (Shaw et al. 2017; Clifford et al. 2019). Label it Emerging whenever you discuss nutrition alongside HSR.
