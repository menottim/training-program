# Returning From a Training Layoff (Detraining and Retraining)

## Scope

This file covers three things. First, what happens to strength and muscle when resistance training stops. Second, how much comes back, and how fast. Third, how to set the restart load when the athlete returns to a lift after a gap.

The file is calibrated for Menotti, a 40-year-old in-season recreational athlete. He has now had three layoffs that changed the programming. The file covers both travel gaps and injury layoffs.

This file does __not__ cover the calf rehab progression itself. See [calf-strain-rehab.md](calf-strain-rehab.md). It does not cover deload strategy in a lifter who is not fatigued. See [in-season-volume.md](in-season-volume.md). It does not cover pre-competition tapering.

## Athlete context

Three layoffs have driven programming decisions:

- __Weeks 3 and 4, March 22 to April 4, 2026.__ Travel. This gave a gap of approximately 20 days from barbell work. He did hotel bodyweight sessions only.
- __Week 5, April 5 to 11, 2026.__ A re-baseline push week after that gap. It also introduced the barbell back squat and the barbell bench press.
- __Weeks 16 to 21, June 21 to July 27, 2026.__ An acute left medial gastrocnemius strain, then travel. The upper-body work continued and kept progressing through the layoff, and the bench press went from 155 to 165. The barbell lower body work stopped completely. The trap bar deadlift and the back squat ran on 6/18 and did not run again until 7/27, a gap of __39 days__.

Each restart load was set ad hoc at the time. This file exists so that the next one is not.

## Mechanism

### Strength lasts longer than size

This is the most useful finding for setting a restart load. Muscle __size__ and muscle __strength__ decay on different clocks.

Fibre cross-sectional area reverses inside 4 weeks (Mujika Part I). In Psilander's human trial it had returned fully to baseline after 20 weeks of detraining. Strength in that same trial stayed approximately 60% above the pre-training value. Mujika Part II reports the same asymmetry in strength athletes past 4 weeks. Fibre areas reduced significantly, and force production declined only slowly. It often stayed above untrained values for very long periods.

The part that survives is largely neural and motor-learning. The skill of the lift lasts longer than the tissue that performs it.

The practical consequence follows. After one or two months off, the athlete will probably look smaller and feel weaker than he actually is. The first sessions back usually go better than he expects. That argues for a __modest__ cut at the restart, and for a re-test rather than an assumption. It does not argue for a dramatic rebuild from the beginning.

### "Muscle memory" has better support in mice than in men

Bruusgaard 2010 is a clean rodent mechanism. Myonuclei gained during overload survive severe atrophy, and they appear to be shielded from the apoptotic activity in inactive muscle. A prior episode of hypertrophy also slowed later disuse atrophy. This is a plausible basis for faster regrowth in tissue that trained before.

Psilander 2019 tested that prediction directly in humans. The design compared one previously trained leg against one untrained leg, and then retrained both. The trained leg did not respond better. The myonuclear number did not change in either leg.

So the mechanism has strong animal support, and the human performance prediction failed in the trial designed to test it. Do not promise a faster regain. Expect __retained strength__ instead. That finding has better support, and it is more useful.

### A break costs less than it feels like it costs

Ogasawara 2013 is the most reassuring result here. Cycles of 3 weeks off and 6 weeks on matched 24 weeks of continuous training, on both CSA and 1RM.

The caveats matter. The study used 14 young men, and it measured the bench press only. The equality also partly reflects the falling rate of gain in continuous training, not a zero cost for detraining.

Even so, the result means a 3-week travel gap is not a catastrophe. It does not justify a restart far below the previous load.

### Ramping back: what is established and what is not

Gabbett's review supports the general shape of the caution. A large and rapid load increase tracks with non-contact soft-tissue injury, and a well-developed chronic load base is protective rather than dangerous. Respect that when the athlete comes off a layoff. A load spike causes exactly the kind of injury this program exists to avoid.

The specific tool from that literature is __not__ supportable. Impellizzeri 2020 took apart the acute:chronic workload ratio. No study has properly estimated a causal effect. The metric mishandles ratio data. It is ambiguous. It does not relate to injury risk consistently or in one direction. Their conclusion is blunt: there is no evidence supporting its use.

So ramp deliberately on general grounds. Do not cite a ratio threshold as a validated rule.

## Practical Application (calibrated for Menotti)

### Restart-load bands

Measure from the last __clean__ working set, which means all prescribed reps at the target RPE. Do not measure from the last session logged, because that session may have been a reduced power day or a contrast day.

| Time off the lift | Restart load | Expected rebuild |
|---|---|---|
| Under 2 weeks | Resume at the last clean load | Normal double progression |
| 2 to 4 weeks | 5 to 10% below | Back to the prior load in 1 to 2 sessions |
| 4 to 8 weeks | 10 to 15% below | Back to the prior load in 2 to 4 sessions |
| Over 8 weeks, or any injury layoff that affected the loaded tissue | 15 to 20% below. Re-test rather than assume. | 3 to 6 sessions, gated on the symptom reads |

__These bands are a judgment call. They are not a published protocol.__ No trial prescribes a restart percentage after a layoff. The bands come from three lines of reasoning: the direction and the dose-dependence of the Bosquet effect sizes; the finding that strength lasts longer than size; and the general caution against a load spike. The bands are deliberately __small__, because the evidence says the athlete keeps more strength than he feels. This is the weakest claim in the file. Revise it against Menotti's own data as more layoffs accumulate.

### Rules that follow from the evidence

- __Measure from the last clean set, not the last logged set.__ In July 2026 the last logged trap bar deadlift was 4x3 at 245 on 6/18, which was a reduced contrast session. The last clean 4x5 at 245 was on 6/11. The clean set is the reference.
- __Cut the load. Keep the movement.__ Mujika Part II shows that adaptations survive when the athlete holds the intensity, cuts the volume markedly, and reduces the frequency only moderately. On a travel week, prefer a few hard sets of the real lifts at close to the normal load. Do not substitute a large volume of easy bodyweight work.
- __Expect a fast climb, then normal progression.__ Two clean sessions earn the standard increment: 10 lb on a lower body lift and 5 lb on an upper body lift. No fractional plates are available. See the Equipment Notes in CLAUDE.md. Do not slow a rebuild that is going well.
- __Do not add a deload after a layoff.__ The layoff already deloaded him. A planned deload in a lifter who is not fatigued costs strength (Coleman 2024, see [in-season-volume.md](in-season-volume.md)). A deload after a detraining period compounds the loss.
- __Separate the deconditioned tissue from the injured tissue.__ After an injury layoff there are two different reasons to reduce load, and each needs a different amount. Barbell lower body work returning after a calf strain is mostly a __deconditioning__ problem, not a calf problem. The trap bar deadlift is hip-dominant. The bottom of a squat holds the knee flexed, which slackens the biarticular gastrocnemius instead of stretching it. See [calf-strain-rehab.md](calf-strain-rehab.md) for the movements that actually load a healing gastrocnemius. Those are plyometrics, sprinting and change of direction. They are not the barbell lifts.
- __Log the morning-after read on the first two or three sessions back.__ It is the cheapest early-warning signal available, and it is the one most often skipped.
- __On travel, the goal is to limit the gap. The goal is not to reproduce the program.__ Even a small amount of hard loading holds the chronic base up, and the chronic base is the protective variable in Gabbett 2016.

### Age note

Bosquet found larger losses in adults over 65. Menotti is 40, so he is not in that band. Do not import the elderly detraining literature, or its urgency. The relevant age adjustment is the recovery capacity, which is approximately 10 to 15% lower than in a younger lifter. The program already accounts for it, through the 3-day lifting schedule and the conservative volume (Lexell 1995, see CLAUDE.md Section 7).

## Unresolved Questions

- __No trial prescribes a restart load after a layoff.__ The band table above is reasoned, not measured. Treat it as a starting heuristic. Correct it against Menotti's own logged results.
- __Human muscle memory is unresolved.__ There is a strong rodent mechanism (Bruusgaard 2010) against a negative human performance test (Psilander 2019, n=19, a single trial). Do not assert it in either direction.
- __Detraining data in trained 40-year-old lifters is thin.__ Bosquet stratified around age 65. Ogasawara used young men. Psilander used young adults. Extrapolation to a trained 40-year-old is an assumption, not a finding, and the primary literature does not flag it.
- __No one knows how much sport offsets lower body detraining.__ Menotti's layoffs are rarely total, and he often continues to play games. No literature converts basketball or hockey exposure into a resistance-training equivalent. [in-season-volume.md](in-season-volume.md) already establishes that no such equivalence exists.
- __Nothing here shows that the upper body and the lower body decay at different rates.__ The upper body work survived the June and July 2026 layoff because he continued to train it. That is an effect of training frequency. It is not evidence of differential retention.

## Corrections Log

- __2026-07-27, when this file was created.__ Week 21 needed restart loads after 39 days off barbell lower body work. That was the third layoff to drive a programming decision. This file corrects a habit rather than a claim: earlier restarts were set ad hoc, session by session. It also records one conflation to avoid. The progression-stall protocol in CLAUDE.md Section 1 says to reset 10% and build back over 2 to 3 weeks. That protocol had become the default model for a layoff restart. A stall and a layoff are different problems. A stall is a fatigue or adaptation ceiling at a load the athlete currently owns. A layoff is decay at a load he no longer owns. The two can produce similar numbers, but the reasoning differs. The bands in this file govern a layoff.
- __2026-07-27.__ Training culture often frames muscle memory as "you regain it faster". The one human trial that tested this directly does not support that framing (Psilander 2019). The defensible version is that the athlete retains strength. It is not that strength returns faster.
