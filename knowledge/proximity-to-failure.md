# Proximity to Failure, Load Intensity, and How Many Hard Sets a Week Actually Buys

## Scope

Covers how close a set should be taken to muscular failure, how that interacts with weekly set volume and load, and what the evidence says about AMRAP or "5+" top sets as a programming tool. Calibrated for a 40-year-old in-season recreational athlete with bilateral midportion Achilles tendinopathy and a healed medial gastrocnemius strain, lifting two days a week, whose priorities in order are injury prevention, then strength and power, then vertical jump. Complements `in-season-volume.md`, which covers weekly set targets, deloads and taper. Does not cover velocity-based training hardware, powerlifting peaking blocks, or hypertrophy-first programming.

__Origin:__ Written 2026-08-14 in response to a direct question: whether the program should shift toward fewer sets at higher effort, with the final set of a lift run as "5+", meaning five reps then continue toward failure.

## Mechanism (what the controlled data actually shows)

### The honest headline: for strength, effort per set is the wrong lever

The intuition that "higher intensity beats more volume" is half right, and which half matters.

Robinson et al. 2024 (Sports Med 54:2209-2231, PMID 38970765) is the most direct test. Rather than the usual binary of failure versus not-failure, they estimated RIR for every effect in the literature and modeled it continuously, adjusting for load, volume-equating method, intervention duration and training status. For strength, every best-fit model produced an RIR slope whose confidence interval contained zero. For hypertrophy, every best-fit model produced a negative slope whose confidence interval excluded zero. Their own conclusion: "Strength gains were similar across a wide range of RIR, while muscle hypertrophy improves as sets are terminated closer to failure."

Grgic et al. 2022 (J Sport Health Sci 11:202-211, PMID 33497853) reached the same place categorically: no significant difference between failure and non-failure for strength (ES -0.09) or hypertrophy (ES 0.22). The subgroup finding is the one worth remembering. In studies that did not equate volume between groups, which is what real training looks like, non-failure training significantly FAVORED strength (ES -0.32, CI -0.57 to -0.07). Read plainly: when you let each group train the way its rule implies, the group that stopped short got stronger.

So if the goal were maximum muscle, taking sets closer to failure is supported. The goal here is strength, power and a vertical jump, and for those outcomes proximity to failure is close to a free variable. What is not free is load. Suchomel et al. 2018 (Sports Med 48:765-785, PMID 29372481) and Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2020 (Sports Med 50:751-765, PMID 31797219) both put the strength stimulus in load and genuine effort at that load, not in the last one or two reps of a set.

### Volume has diminishing returns, and they bite strength harder than size

Pelland et al. 2026 (Sports Med 56:481-505, PMID 41343037) meta-regressed weekly set volume and frequency across 67 studies and 2058 participants, after classifying every contributing set as direct or indirect to the measured outcome. Volume raised both outcomes with 100% posterior probability, but both best-fit models showed diminishing returns, and the paper is explicit that the diminishing returns for strength are "considerably more pronounced." Frequency was the mirror image: a consistently identifiable positive effect on strength, and effects compatible with negligible for hypertrophy.

Two consequences for a two-day-a-week lifter chasing 5RM numbers. First, adding sets to a strength lift buys progressively less, so a set-count ceiling costs less than it looks like it should. Second, the variable that would help most, frequency, is the one the calendar has already capped. On two days a week the honest move is to protect load and per-session quality rather than to pile sets onto the two days available.

Caveat worth stating: the mean participant age in Pelland was 25.2 ± 5.2 years, and Grgic's included studies were all young adults. Transfer to 40 is an assumption, not a finding.

### Failure has a recovery price, and it is measurable in hours

Refalo et al. 2023 (Sports Med Open 9:10, PMID 36752989) had 24 trained lifters do six bench press sets at 75% 1RM to failure, to 1-RIR, or to 3-RIR, then tracked velocity recovery. Four minutes post-session: failure down 25%, 1-RIR down 13%, 3-RIR down 8%. At 24 hours: failure and 1-RIR still down 3%, 3-RIR up 2%. By 48 hours the differences were gone.

That 24-to-48-hour window is the operative number for anyone lifting near a game. It is the reason a hard set placed on Friday and a hard set placed on Thursday are not the same prescription when games are Sunday.

Refalo et al. 2024 (J Sports Sci 42:85-101, PMID 38393985) ran the training study version: 8 weeks, two sessions a week, in an unusually experienced sample, 1-2 RIR versus momentary failure. Hypertrophy was similar. The mechanism note matters more than the outcome: failure produced so much repetition loss from first set to last that the 1-2 RIR condition accumulated a similar volume load anyway. Going to failure on set one buys you a worse set three.

### The jump-specific evidence points away from failure

This is the finding most relevant to priority number three. Pareja-Blanco et al. 2017 (Scand J Med Sci Sports 27:724-735, PMID 27038416) trained 22 young men on the squat for 8 weeks at identical loads (70% progressing to 85% 1RM), differing only in when each set was stopped: at 20% velocity loss or at 40%. A 40% velocity loss in the squat means most sets end at or very near failure. A 20% loss means roughly half the available reps.

Squat 1RM gains were similar. Countermovement jump improved 9.5% in the 20% group versus 3.5% in the 40% group (P < 0.05), and the 20% group did 40% fewer total reps to get there. The 40% group gained more vastus lateralis and intermedius size, and lost myosin heavy chain IIx percentage, which the 20% group preserved. Fast-fiber phenotype and jump output were protected by stopping earlier, at no cost to maximal strength.

Fragala et al. 2019 (NSCA position statement, J Strength Cond Res 33:2019-2052, PMID 31343601) says the same thing prescriptively for older trainees: for power development, do not take repetitions to concentric failure.

### Where the "5+" set genuinely earns its place

None of the above says never approach failure. It says the payoff is informational rather than adaptive.

Halperin et al. 2022 (Sports Med 52:377-390, PMID 34542869) meta-analyzed 262 effect sizes across 12 studies and 414 participants and found lifters underpredict reps to failure by about 0.95 reps on average (95% CI 0.17 to 1.73), with heterogeneity so high (I-squared 97.9%) that the individual error is the real story. Accuracy improved closer to failure, with heavier loads, and in later sets. In other words: an RIR-prescribed program drifts, systematically toward too easy, and the drift is invisible from inside the program.

An occasional AMRAP fixes exactly that. Take the last set of five and keep going: the rep count tells you what the RIR target has actually been, and it produces an estimated 1RM that makes the next load decision arithmetic instead of a guess. That is the defensible case for "5+", and it is a calibration and progression case, not a hypertrophy or strength-per-set case. It also happens to fit a two-day week, where a lift may only be trained once and there is no second session that week to correct a bad load guess.

The cost is the Refalo fatigue clock above, which is why the tool belongs on one lift per session rather than on every lift, and on the training day farther from the next game.

### The calf and tendon work is the exception, and it already runs near failure

Beyer et al. 2015 (Am J Sports Med 43:1704-1711, PMID 26018970) prescribes HSR by repetition maximum: 15RM in week 1, then 12RM, 10RM, 8RM, and 6RM by weeks 9-12, three sets, three times a week, six-second tempo. An XRM prescription is by definition a set taken to or very near failure. Morrison and Cook 2022 (Sports Med 52:1219-1222) push the point further: if every rep feels easy, the protocol is underloaded and the tendon is not getting the signal.

So the tendon work is the one place in this program where near-failure effort is the protocol rather than a choice, and it is also where the program has historically failed by running easy. Keep the calf HSR at genuine XRM effort. Take the RIR discipline off the calves and put it on the barbell lifts.

## Practical Application

1. __Separate the two variables in every prescription.__ Load intensity (percent of 1RM) is the strength lever and stays high. Proximity to failure (RIR) is a fatigue-management lever and stays moderate on compounds. Saying "train harder" without saying which one is meant is how a program ends up grinding at loads that are too light.

2. __Compound barbell lifts: 2-3 RIR as the standing prescription.__ Robinson 2024 says strength does not care, Refalo 2023 says 3-RIR is functionally recovered at 24 hours, and Pareja-Blanco 2017 says stopping earlier protects jump and fast-fiber phenotype. There is no strength argument for routinely grinding trap bar deadlifts or squats to failure.

3. __One "+" set per session, on one lift, on the day farther from the game.__ Run the final set of that lift as reps-plus, stopping when a rep visibly slows or form changes rather than at true mechanical failure. Use the rep count two ways: to check whether the RIR target has been honest (Halperin), and to set the next load via an estimated 1RM. With games on Sunday, this belongs on the earlier of the two lifting days, not the one 40 hours out.

4. __Do not run "+" sets on both main lifts of the same day.__ Two AMRAPs on one lower-body day is the fatigue profile of failure training with none of its hypertrophy justification, and Refalo 2024's repetition-loss finding says the second one will be a worse set anyway.

5. __Cap the set count per pattern rather than chasing volume.__ Pelland's diminishing returns for strength are pronounced, so on two lifting days per week roughly 4-6 hard sets per week per main pattern captures most of the strength return. Spend the freed capacity on load and on the tendon work, not on more sets.

6. __Isolation, machine and cable work is where true failure is cheap.__ Woodchops, Pallof presses, pulldowns and leg extensions carry little systemic fatigue and no barbell-under-load risk. If the urge is to train hard to failure, spend it here.

7. __Never run a "+" set on a power exposure.__ Box jumps, depth drops and any jump work stay far from failure by definition. Fragala 2019 is explicit, and Pareja-Blanco 2017 shows fatigue accumulation actively costs jump output.

8. __Keep the calf HSR at true XRM effort.__ Beyer's protocol is a repetition-maximum prescription and Morrison and Cook 2022 warn that most published HSR underloads. This is the one block where "the last rep should be hard" is the actual instruction.

## Unresolved or Bleeding-Edge Questions

- __Age transfer.__ Pelland's sample averaged 25 years old and Grgic's included studies were all young adults. There is no proximity-to-failure trial in 40-to-50-year-olds. A 2023 scoping review of recovery from resistance exercise in older adults only included participants 65 and over and found the evidence too heterogeneous to recommend frequencies or volumes. The 40-to-64 bracket is close to an evidence vacuum, so the conservative reading is that the fatigue cost of failure is at least as large at 40 as in the young samples, not smaller.
- __How much of the hypertrophy-to-failure effect is real.__ Robinson 2024's authors explicitly caution that their RIR values were estimated from study descriptions rather than measured, and that between-participant and between-set RIR variability is large. Refalo 2023's meta found the failure advantage for hypertrophy trivial (ES 0.19) with a lower CI bound sitting on zero.
- __Whether an AMRAP-derived estimated 1RM is accurate enough to program from.__ Rep-max prediction equations degrade above roughly 10 reps and have not been validated specifically in trap bar deadlift. Treat the estimate as a trend line across weeks, not a true max.
- __Velocity loss as a stopping rule without hardware.__ Pareja-Blanco's 20% threshold is the cleanest available operationalization of "stop moderately short," but implementing it needs a velocity device. RIR is the practical proxy and it is the noisier of the two.
- __Interaction with tendinopathy specifically.__ No trial has tested proximity to failure on tendon outcomes. The HSR literature prescribes XRM loading, which implies near-failure, but whether that is load-driven or effort-driven for tenocyte signaling is unresolved.
