# Proximity to Failure, Load Intensity, and How Many Hard Sets a Week Actually Buys

## Scope

This file covers three topics. First, how close a set should go to muscular failure. Second, how that interacts with weekly set volume and with load. Third, what the evidence says about an AMRAP set, or a "5+" top set, as a programming tool.

The file is calibrated for a 40-year-old in-season recreational athlete. He has bilateral midportion Achilles tendinopathy and a healed medial gastrocnemius strain. He lifts two days per week. His priorities, in order, are injury prevention, then strength and power, then vertical jump.

This file works with `in-season-volume.md`, which covers the weekly set targets, deloads and tapers. This file does not cover velocity-based training hardware, powerlifting peaking blocks, or hypertrophy-first programming.

__Origin.__ This file was written on 2026-08-14, after a direct question. The question was whether the program should move toward fewer sets at higher effort, with the final set of a lift run as "5+". A "5+" set means five reps, then more reps toward failure.

## Mechanism (what the controlled data actually shows)

### The honest headline: for strength, effort per set is the wrong lever

The idea that "higher intensity beats more volume" is half correct. Which half is correct matters.

Robinson et al. 2024 (Sports Med 54:2209-2231, PMID 38970765) is the most direct test. Earlier work used a binary comparison of failure against non-failure. This paper instead estimated the RIR for every effect in the literature and modeled it as a continuous variable. The models adjusted for load, for the volume-equating method, for the intervention duration, and for training status. For strength, every best-fit model produced an RIR slope whose confidence interval contained zero. For hypertrophy, every best-fit model produced a negative slope whose confidence interval excluded zero. The authors concluded: "Strength gains were similar across a wide range of RIR, while muscle hypertrophy improves as sets are terminated closer to failure."

Grgic et al. 2022 (J Sport Health Sci 11:202-211, PMID 33497853) reached the same conclusion with a categorical comparison. There was no significant difference between failure and non-failure for strength (ES -0.09) or for hypertrophy (ES 0.22). The subgroup finding is the one to remember. Some studies did not equate the volume between the groups, which is what real training looks like. In those studies, training short of failure significantly FAVORED strength (ES -0.32, CI -0.57 to -0.07). Stated plainly: each group trained as its own rule implied, and the group that stopped short got stronger.

So the evidence supports sets closer to failure if the goal is maximum muscle. The goals here are strength, power and a vertical jump. For those outcomes, proximity to failure is almost a free variable. Load is not free. Suchomel et al. 2018 (Sports Med 48:765-785, PMID 29372481) and Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2020 (Sports Med 50:751-765, PMID 31797219) both locate the strength stimulus in the load, and in genuine effort at that load. Neither locates it in the last one or two reps of a set.

### Volume has diminishing returns, and they bite strength harder than size

Pelland et al. 2026 (Sports Med 56:481-505, PMID 41343037) meta-regressed weekly set volume and frequency across 67 studies and 2058 participants. First they classified every contributing set as direct or indirect to the measured outcome. Volume increased both outcomes, with a posterior probability of 100%. Both best-fit models also showed diminishing returns. The paper states that the diminishing returns for strength are "considerably more pronounced". Frequency showed the opposite pattern: a consistent positive effect on strength, and an effect on hypertrophy that is compatible with zero.

This has two consequences for a lifter who trains two days per week and wants higher 5RM numbers. First, each set added to a strength lift returns progressively less, so a cap on the set count costs less than it appears to. Second, frequency would help the most, and the calendar has already capped the frequency. On two days per week, protect the load and the quality of each session. Do not add sets to the two available days.

One caveat: the mean participant age in Pelland was 25.2 ± 5.2 years, and every study Grgic included used young adults. Transfer to age 40 is an assumption. It is not a finding.

### Failure has a recovery cost, and that cost is measurable in hours

Refalo et al. 2023 (Sports Med Open 9:10, PMID 36752989) tested 24 trained lifters. Each lifter did six bench press sets at 75% of 1RM, in three separate conditions: to failure, to 1 RIR, and to 3 RIR. The researchers then tracked velocity recovery. Four minutes after the session, velocity was 25% down after failure, 13% down at 1 RIR, and 8% down at 3 RIR. At 24 hours, failure and 1 RIR were still 3% down, and 3 RIR was 2% up. By 48 hours the differences were gone.

The window from 24 to 48 hours is the operative number for anyone who lifts near a game. It is why a hard set on Friday and a hard set on Thursday are different prescriptions when the games fall on Sunday.

Refalo et al. 2024 (J Sports Sci 42:85-101, PMID 38393985) ran the training version of that study. It lasted 8 weeks, at two sessions per week, in an unusually experienced sample. It compared 1 to 2 RIR against momentary failure. Hypertrophy was similar in both conditions. The mechanism matters more than the outcome. Failure caused so much repetition loss from the first set to the last set that the 1 to 2 RIR condition accumulated a similar volume load. A set taken to failure first gives you a worse third set.

### The jump-specific evidence points away from failure

This finding is the most relevant one for priority number three. Pareja-Blanco et al. 2017 (Scand J Med Sci Sports 27:724-735, PMID 27038416) trained 22 young men on the squat for 8 weeks. Both groups used identical loads, which progressed from 70% to 85% of 1RM. Only one variable differed: where each set stopped. One group stopped at 20% velocity loss. The other stopped at 40%. A 40% velocity loss in the squat ends most sets at or very near failure. A 20% loss uses approximately half the available reps.

Squat 1RM gains were similar. The countermovement jump improved 9.5% in the 20% group and 3.5% in the 40% group (P < 0.05). The 20% group also did 40% fewer total reps. The 40% group gained more size in the vastus lateralis and the vastus intermedius. It also lost myosin heavy chain IIx percentage, and the 20% group kept that percentage. The earlier stopping point therefore protected both the fast-fiber phenotype and the jump output. It cost nothing in maximal strength.

Fragala et al. 2019 (NSCA position statement, J Strength Cond Res 33:2019-2052, PMID 31343601) gives the same instruction for an older trainee. For power development, do not take repetitions to concentric failure.

### Where the "5+" set does earn its place

Nothing above says to avoid failure completely. It says the benefit is informational, not adaptive.

Halperin et al. 2022 (Sports Med 52:377-390, PMID 34542869) meta-analyzed 262 effect sizes across 12 studies and 414 participants. Lifters underestimated their remaining reps by approximately 0.95 reps (95% CI 0.17 to 1.73). The heterogeneity was very high, at I-squared 97.9%, so the individual error is the real finding. Accuracy improved closer to failure, at heavier loads, and in later sets. Stated another way: an RIR-prescribed program drifts systematically toward loads that are too light, and no one inside the program can see that drift.

An occasional AMRAP set corrects it. Take the last set of five and continue. The rep count then shows what the RIR target has actually been. It also gives an estimated 1RM, which turns the next load decision into arithmetic instead of a guess. That is the defensible case for a "5+" set. It is a case about calibration and progression. It is not a case about hypertrophy or about strength per set. The tool also suits a two-day week, where a lift may run only once and no second session exists that week to correct a bad load guess.

The cost is the Refalo fatigue clock above. That cost is why the tool belongs on one lift per session, not on every lift, and on the training day farther from the next game.

### The calf and tendon work is the exception, and it already runs near failure

Beyer et al. 2015 (Am J Sports Med 43:1704-1711, PMID 26018970) prescribes HSR by repetition maximum. The load runs 15RM in week 1, then 12RM, then 10RM, then 8RM, and 6RM by weeks 9 to 12. The dose is three sets, three times per week, at a six-second tempo. An XRM prescription is a set taken to failure, or very near it. Morrison and Cook 2022 (Sports Med 52:1219-1222) go further: if every rep feels easy, the protocol is underloaded and the tendon is not getting the signal.

The tendon work is therefore the one place in this program where near-failure effort is the protocol, not a choice. It is also the place where this program has failed in the past, by running too easy. Keep the calf HSR at genuine XRM effort. Apply the RIR discipline to the barbell lifts instead, not to the calves.

## Practical Application

1. __Separate the two variables in every prescription.__ Load intensity, as a percent of 1RM, is the strength lever. Keep it high. Proximity to failure, measured as RIR, is a fatigue-management lever. Keep it moderate on the compound lifts. An instruction to "train harder" does not say which variable to change. That ambiguity is how a program ends up grinding out sets at loads that are too light.

2. __Prescribe 2 to 3 RIR on the compound barbell lifts. This is the standing rule.__ Robinson 2024 shows that strength does not respond to RIR. Refalo 2023 shows that 3 RIR is functionally recovered at 24 hours. Pareja-Blanco 2017 shows that an earlier stopping point protects both the jump and the fast-fiber phenotype. There is no strength argument for a routine grind to failure on the trap bar deadlift or the squat.

3. __Use one reps-plus set per session. Put it on one lift. Put it on the day farther from the game.__ Run the final set of that lift as reps-plus. Stop when a rep slows visibly or the form changes. Do not go to true mechanical failure. Use the rep count for two purposes. First, check whether the RIR target has been accurate (Halperin). Second, set the next load from an estimated 1RM. The games fall on Sunday, so put this set on the earlier of the two lifting days. Do not put it on the day 40 hours before the game.

4. __Do not put a reps-plus set on both main lifts of one day.__ Two AMRAP sets on one lower body day give the fatigue profile of failure training and none of its hypertrophy justification. Refalo 2024 found large repetition loss after a set to failure, so the second AMRAP will also be a worse set.

5. __Cap the set count for each pattern. Do not chase volume.__ Pelland found pronounced diminishing returns for strength. On two lifting days per week, approximately 4 to 6 hard sets per week for each main pattern captures most of the strength return. Spend the released capacity on load and on the tendon work. Do not spend it on more sets.

6. __Take isolation, machine and cable work to true failure. It is cheap there.__ Woodchops, Pallof presses, pulldowns and leg extensions cause little systemic fatigue. They also carry no risk from a barbell under load. Put the hard effort here.

7. __Never run a reps-plus set on a power exposure.__ Box jumps, depth drops and all other jump work stay far from failure. Fragala 2019 states this directly. Pareja-Blanco 2017 shows that accumulated fatigue reduces jump output.

8. __Keep the calf HSR at true XRM effort.__ The Beyer protocol prescribes a repetition maximum. Morrison and Cook 2022 warn that most published HSR protocols underload the tendon. This is the one block where the instruction is that the last rep must be hard.

## Unresolved or Bleeding-Edge Questions

- __Transfer to age 40.__ Pelland's sample averaged 25 years old. Every study Grgic included used young adults. No proximity-to-failure trial exists in people aged 40 to 50. A 2023 scoping review of recovery from resistance exercise in older adults included only participants aged 65 and above. It found the evidence too heterogeneous to support a recommendation on frequency or volume. The bracket from 40 to 64 is almost empty of evidence. The conservative reading is that failure costs at least as much fatigue at age 40 as in the young samples. It does not cost less.
- __How much of the hypertrophy advantage from failure is real.__ The authors of Robinson 2024 caution that they estimated the RIR values from study descriptions. They did not measure them. They also note that RIR varies widely between participants and between sets. The Refalo 2023 meta-analysis found the failure advantage for hypertrophy trivial (ES 0.19), and its lower confidence limit sat on zero.
- __Whether an estimated 1RM from an AMRAP set is accurate enough to program from.__ Rep-max prediction equations become unreliable above approximately 10 reps. No one has validated them specifically for the trap bar deadlift. Use the estimate as a trend across weeks. Do not use it as a true maximum.
- __Velocity loss as a stopping rule without hardware.__ The 20% threshold from Pareja-Blanco is the clearest available definition of "stop moderately short". It needs a velocity device to apply. RIR is the practical substitute, and it is the noisier of the two measures.
- __The interaction with tendinopathy.__ No trial has tested proximity to failure against a tendon outcome. The HSR literature prescribes XRM loading, which implies near-failure effort. It is unresolved whether load or effort drives the tenocyte signal.

## Corrections Log

- __2026-08-14:__ This file was created. It corrects and refines three claims elsewhere in the repository:
  - __The RPE targets in CLAUDE.md Section 1.__ Phase 3 read "RPE 7-9 on primary lifts". Evidence does not support the RPE 9 end of that range for strength, and it costs measurable velocity for 24 hours (Refalo 2023, PMID 36752989). Change the standing compound prescription to RPE 7 to 8, which is 2 to 3 RIR. Reserve RPE 9 and above for a deliberate calibration set.
  - __How `in-season-volume.md` describes effort.__ That file states that low-volume high-effort training produces strength gains similar to higher-volume moderate-effort training. That statement is accurate. Do not read it as an argument for training near failure. Robinson 2024 and Grgic 2022 both found strength unaffected by proximity to failure. In Grgic's volume-non-equated subgroup, training short of failure favored strength (ES -0.32).
  - __Vertical jump and effort.__ CLAUDE.md Section 6 covers plyometric dosing. It did not state that the end point of a barbell set affects the jump. Pareja-Blanco 2017 (PMID 27038416) stopped squat sets at either 20% or 40% velocity loss. The 20% group gained 9.5% in the countermovement jump and the 40% group gained 3.5%. Both groups gained the same 1RM. Where a squat set ends is therefore a vertical-jump variable, not only a fatigue variable.
