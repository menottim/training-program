# Ice Hockey Skating: Calf and Achilles Load Profile

## Scope

This file covers one narrow question: does ice hockey skating load the gastrocnemius, soleus and Achilles complex in a way relevant to this athlete's bilateral Achilles tendinopathy and his 2026-06-21 gastrocnemius strain. It does not attempt a hockey-to-lifting-sets conversion; no such conversion exists for any sport (`in-season-volume.md`).

The file is calibrated for a 40-year-old recreational athlete who plays hockey once per week, typically Sunday PM, alongside basketball.

## Key Findings

| Claim | Evidence tier | Primary source |
|---|---|---|
| Skating push-off drives through ankle plantarflexion, with the calf muscles active during both the propulsive and glide phases of the stride. | Moderate (EMG and kinematic study, n=10) | Buckeridge et al. 2015, PLOS One, PMID 25973775 |
| High-caliber skaters show ankle dorsiflexion up to approximately 20-30 degrees during the glide and recovery phase, with plantarflexion below approximately 10 degrees at push-off. | Moderate (kinematic study) | Buckeridge et al. 2015, PMID 25973775; Lafontaine 2007, Sports Biomech, PMID 17933200 |
| The classic acute gastrocnemius strain mechanism is sudden knee extension combined with ankle dorsiflexion at explosive push-off. | Strong | Dixon 2009, PMID 19468870 (already cited in `calf-strain-rehab.md`) |
| No published study measures ice hockey skating's calf or Achilles load directly against basketball, and no published study reports gastrocnemius strain epidemiology specific to ice hockey. | N/A (absence of evidence) | Search conducted 2026-09-11; see Unresolved Questions |

## Mechanism

Skating push-off is a repeated, sub-maximal plantarflexion effort rather than a single ballistic extension. Buckeridge 2015 (PMID 25973775) recorded medial gastrocnemius EMG during on-ice skating and found it active through both the propulsive (ice-contact) phase and the glide phase, consistent with a sustained rather than purely explosive demand. The ankle moves through a larger dorsiflexion range during glide and recovery (approximately 20-30 degrees) than it reaches plantarflexion at push-off (below approximately 10 degrees), per Buckeridge and the companion kinematic studies (Lafontaine 2007, PMID 17933200; Upjohn et al. 2008, PMID 18610773; Stidwill et al. 2010, PMID 20446640).

This is a different loading pattern from the strain's own mechanism. The 6/21 injury followed the classic tennis-leg mechanism: sudden knee extension combined with ankle dorsiflexion at an explosive, single push-off (Dixon 2009, PMID 19468870, already the anchor citation in `calf-strain-rehab.md`). Repeated skating strides are not a single ballistic event, and no source reviewed here reports the knee-extension-plus-dorsiflexion combination as characteristic of the skating stride itself. The mechanism overlap is therefore partial: the calf is under real, repeated load during skating, but the specific injurious combination has not been shown to be a routine feature of the stride.

## Practical Application

1. Treat hockey as a genuine calf and Achilles loading exposure, consistent with the existing CLAUDE.md wording that a hockey session gives "a moderate to high lower body and core stimulus." This file adds a mechanistic basis for that description; it does not change the number, because there is no number to give.
2. Do not treat hockey as equivalent to, or a substitute for, HSR. Buckeridge's EMG data describes activation during unloaded (bodyweight) skating, not a resisted heavy-slow load. It says nothing about whether skating provides a training stimulus comparable to Beyer-protocol HSR.
3. Do not claim hockey directly re-creates the strain mechanism. The available kinematic data does not describe a knee-extension-plus-dorsiflexion push-off pattern as routine in skating. State the calf demand and the injury history as two separate facts rather than asserting a causal single mechanism.

## Unresolved or Bleeding-Edge Questions

- No study converts a hockey game, or a skating stride, into an equivalent number or intensity of resistance-training sets. This is the same gap `in-season-volume.md` documents for basketball, and it holds here too.
- No hockey-specific gastrocnemius or calf strain epidemiology was found in this search. The general strain literature (`calf-strain-rehab.md`) draws on Australian football, soccer, running and tennis, not hockey.
- Whether skating's dorsiflexion-heavy glide phase carries a meaningfully different Achilles tendon load than basketball's jump-landing pattern is not established. This file cannot answer it and does not attempt to.
